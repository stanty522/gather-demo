## Section 1: Demand Snapshot

### Demand Signal

The Second Chance / Returning Citizen movement has crossed from social service infrastructure into organized identity politics. "Returning citizen," "justice-impacted," and "second chance" have become explicit self-labels, not euphemisms assigned by case workers. FICPFM (Formerly Incarcerated People's Policy Forum), the Center for Employment Opportunities, and networks like the Second Chance Business Coalition (with 100+ employer signatories including JPMorgan Chase, Walmart, and Accenture) have built visible institutional infrastructure. The identity is no longer confined to advocacy circles — it has entered HR, consumer markets, and community commerce.

The wireless dimension is structural. Connectivity is a compliance requirement, not a preference: most state parole and supervised release conditions mandate that parolees maintain contact availability for check-ins, GPS monitoring compliance, and employment verification. Without a functioning phone plan, reintegration stalls. The population acquires wireless service at a specific, predictable moment (Day 0–1 post-release) and does so from a state of zero — no existing plan, no credit history in many cases, no retail presence inside the facility. The carrier that meets them at that moment, through a channel they trust, wins the acquisition with no competition.

### Trigger

TR-02: Incarceration Release / Reentry. Day of release or within 24–48 hours post-release. The trigger is a forced transition — the individual has no wireless plan because phone access was restricted during incarceration. This is a greenfield acquisition event with Intent Score 5.0, the highest in the Trigger Event Library. There is no plan to abandon, no number to port in most cases, and no incumbent carrier defending the relationship.

### Addressable Population

- **Total formerly incarcerated living in community (U.S.):** 5M+ (Bureau of Justice Statistics / ASPE, 2024)
- **Annual releases from state and federal prisons:** ~600K per year (BJS, 2023)
- **Active Second Chance identity advocates** (community-engaged, using self-labels, participating in organized networks): estimated 1–2M (FICPFM network size, Second Chance employer program participant counts, and reentry nonprofit client bases; Gather internal estimation methodology, 2026)
- **Switching-eligible in any 24-month window** (the MT-09 demand window): ~1.2M releases over two years plus advocate-identity growth among the existing 5M community population

### Window Duration

**24–36 months.** The window is driven by two overlapping dynamics:

1. **Community formation window (MT-09):** The identity infrastructure is being built now. Reentry advocacy organizations are professionalizing, employer coalitions are expanding, and a commercial ecosystem around second-chance hiring is taking shape. The 24–36 month window is the period during which a brand can enter as a founding partner and earn community credibility ahead of any competition.
2. **Annual acquisition flow:** Unlike some windows that close entirely, this segment has continuous replenishment via ~600K annual releases. The window is about competitive timing, not demand expiry. Being first to build channel partnerships with reentry organizations (Safer Foundation, CEO, Goodwill reentry programs) creates a durable first-mover channel advantage that is hard to displace once established.

**Decay indicator:** Window urgency increases if any major MVNO (TracFone parent Verizon, Boost parent Dish) moves to formalize reentry organization partnerships. Currently no evidence of this — the window is open.

## Section 2: Core Factor Analysis

### Persona: Marcus

**Age:** 34 | **Location:** Chicago, IL | **Background:** Released 8 months ago after 6 years in state prison. Now working at a construction firm under the Safer Foundation employment program. Active in his reentry community, recently started attending FICPFM community calls. Describes himself as a "returning citizen," not an "ex-con." Uses his phone for parole check-ins, job communications, and staying in his recovery support group.

---

### Factor 2: Spending and Financial Habits

**Quantitative context:**

- Median income for formerly incarcerated individuals in the first year post-release: ~$10,090 annually, approximately half of the general population median (Prison Policy Initiative, 2021)
- 49% of formerly incarcerated individuals are unemployed in the first year post-release (Prison Policy Initiative, 2021)
- Average time to first employment post-release: 3–6 months (Urban Institute, 2020)
- Wireless spend share: Formerly incarcerated individuals overwhelmingly use prepaid no-contract plans; TracFone and Boost dominate the default market through institutional familiarity and cash-payment accessibility (GSMA Intelligence / Prison Policy Initiative analysis, 2022)
- Lifeline program penetration: ~10–15% of eligible formerly incarcerated individuals actively enrolled (FCC Lifeline participation data, 2024)
- Financial account access: 68% of formerly incarcerated individuals are unbanked or underbanked at release (FDIC Survey of Household Use of Banking and Financial Services, 2023)
- Average monthly wireless spend: $30–$45 for this segment based on prepaid plan pricing; $25–$35 after Lifeline subsidy if enrolled (MVNO industry data cross-referenced with Prison Policy Initiative income modeling)

**Interview — 5 questions**

**Q2.1: Walk me through how you handle your phone bill every month.**

Honestly, cash is just easier right now. I don't have a credit card — I had to restart from nothing when I got out. I buy a top-up at the corner store when I get paid, usually around the first. I'm on a $35 plan, which barely gets me through if I'm not on WiFi. Some months I run out of data before the month's done. I'm not proud of it, but it's what I can manage right now.

**Q2.2: When you got out, how did you figure out which phone plan to get?**

The reentry counselor at Safer told me to go grab a TracFone from Walgreens. That was basically it. Nobody sat down with me and said, here are your options, here's what Lifeline can do for you — I found out about Lifeline weeks later through someone in my group. I think a lot of people are just grabbing whatever is in front of them because you need a phone that same day. Day one, you have no phone and parole needs a number before you leave.

**Q2.3: Is there anything you'd pay more for if the plan was right?**

If something covered me for check-ins without the data cutting out, and I knew it came from people who actually worked with reentry programs — not just a random carrier — I'd probably pay $5 or $10 more a month. What I wouldn't do is sign a contract or pay a deposit. That's off the table. I need to know I can leave if things change. But no, I'm not looking for cheap exactly. I'm looking for something that works and doesn't treat me like a problem.

**Q2.4: Do you ever feel like your current plan was designed for someone else?**

Oh yeah. The TracFone thing — you can tell it's for people who don't have any other options. There's no community. The packaging is the same as what you'd find in an airport convenience store. It works, I guess, but it doesn't feel like anything. Like they just needed to put a product somewhere. I'm not a customer to them, I'm just a transaction. I think about that sometimes because I care about where my money goes now. I didn't used to.

**Q2.5: How do you think about the Lifeline subsidy — is it something you'd use long-term or just a bridge?**

It felt weird at first — like admitting I needed help. I used to be embarrassed to even look into it. But someone in my group reframed it: you're coming out of a system that took six years from you, use every resource available. I think of it as a bridge. Once I'm making better money I want to pay my own way, full price, for something I chose because it's good. Not something I'm on because it's subsidized.

### Factor 3: Needs and Aspirations

**Quantitative context:**

- 75% of employers conduct background checks; 60–70% would likely not hire a person with a criminal record for most positions, though this is changing under Ban the Box and fair-chance hiring legislation (SHRM, 2023)
- Second Chance Business Coalition: 100+ major employers committing to fair-chance hiring (JPMorgan Chase Business Coalition data, 2024)
- Self-labeling adoption: "Returning citizen" and "justice-impacted" usage has increased measurably in advocacy and professional contexts; FICPFM reports that a majority of program participants prefer "returning citizen" over "formerly incarcerated" or "ex-offender" (FICPFM community survey, 2023)
- Recidivism and social connection: Employment, stable housing, and positive social ties are the three strongest protective factors against recidivism (Desistance from Crime literature review, National Institute of Justice, 2023)
- Phone connectivity and reentry outcomes: 93% of reentry case managers identify reliable phone access as critical for employment and compliance outcomes (Reentry Council of the National Association of Counties, 2022)

**Interview — 5 questions**

**Q3.1: What does "returning citizen" mean to you? Why do you use that phrase?**

It means I'm not defined by what happened. I made choices and the system responded — but that's not all I am, and it's not what I'm going to be. "Returning citizen" says I left, I paid my debt, and I'm back. Back with rights, back with responsibilities. "Ex-con" or "felon" — those words put you in a box and let people decide you before you open your mouth. I chose to reject that. There's a whole community of people who have made the same choice, and that community matters to me.

**Q3.2: What do you need your phone to do for you — practically speaking — that most people don't think about?**

It's different from most people. I have parole check-ins — I need to be reachable and able to confirm my location on specific days. I can't miss that. Miss it and you're looking at a violation. Then there's the job: my site supervisor needs to be able to reach me before I show up. I'm also in a recovery support group and we text constantly. And I'm reconnecting with my kids — every call matters. My phone isn't entertainment. It's literally how I stay out of trouble and rebuild my life.

**Q3.3: What do you wish a phone company understood about people like you?**

That we're serious. That we're not high-risk customers who might disappear — we're people who need to prove we're reliable, every single day. We have more riding on staying connected than the average customer. I've never missed a bill since I got out. I've never missed a check-in. What I wish is that a carrier would see that and maybe build something that acknowledges it. Something that says: we know what you're going through, we're here for it, and we're not going to nickel-and-dime you.

**Q3.4: Is there an identity you're trying to build — who do you want to be in five years?**

Construction crew leader, maybe getting my own license eventually. I want to be the guy my kids call when something goes wrong. I want to be somebody in my community — someone who came back and made it and helps other people do the same. I'm already doing some peer mentoring at Safer. That feels like purpose. I think the "returning citizen" thing is part of it — not just a label but a community I'm building something inside of. I want to be someone my younger self would've looked up to.

**Q3.5: What does it feel like when a brand — any brand — actually recognizes your experience?**

It's rare, so it stands out. I joined the Second Chance Business Coalition job fair and one of the employers said, right up front: we recruit from reentry programs on purpose because you bring something most people don't — you know how to survive, you know how to focus, you don't take anything for granted. That hit different. I've never had a phone company say anything like that. They just ask for a deposit. Imagine if someone said: we built this for returning citizens, not despite your history but because of who you are. I'd be loyal for life.

### Factor 4: Fears and Pain Points

**Quantitative context:**

- Cost of losing phone service for a parolee: A missed parole check-in due to no phone service can constitute a technical violation, triggering a warrant or return to custody. 37 states allow incarceration for technical parole violations (Robina Institute, 2022)
- Credit barrier: 70%+ of formerly incarcerated individuals cannot pass standard carrier credit checks, limiting access to postpaid plans (Prison Policy Initiative, 2022)
- Identity documentation gaps: 40–50% of people leaving prison lack government-issued photo ID at the time of release — a prerequisite for most carrier sign-up processes (National Inventory of the Collateral Consequences of Conviction, NIC, 2023)
- Phone loss/theft vulnerability: Formerly incarcerated individuals are more likely to live in transitional housing or shelter environments with higher theft risk; no contract replacement or insurance is common in the prepaid segment
- Digital history gaps: Many returning citizens have thin or no digital footprint — no email address history, no online account history — making standard identity verification processes (required by most carriers) a barrier

**Interview — 5 questions**

**Q4.1: What's your single biggest fear around your phone — not losing it, but what happens if the service fails?**

Missing my parole check-in. Full stop. If my data runs out and I can't confirm my location or reach my parole officer when they call, that's a violation. That's not a bad month — that's potentially going back. I've had nights where I was rationing data at the end of the month because I needed to save it just in case someone from the parole office tried to reach me. That kind of stress — most people never deal with it. Most people just run out of data and shrug.

**Q4.2: When you first tried to get a plan after release, what got in the way?**

I had no ID the day I walked out — they gave me my release paperwork but I had to go get a state ID, which took almost two weeks. So I was trying to get a phone with just my release document and most places wouldn't accept it. TracFone was the only one that asked fewer questions. The Walgreens lady basically just activated the SIM. I needed something that same day. The whole carrier system isn't built for someone who literally just walked out with nothing. It's built for people who already have everything set up.

**Q4.3: Do you worry about how your phone activity might be used — by parole, by employers, by anyone?**

I'm careful. I have to be. Not because I'm doing anything wrong, but because I know how things get interpreted. I know parole can request location data. I know employers Google everything. I try to keep my social media positive — I post about the job, about my kids, about things I'm grateful for. I don't post anything that could be read the wrong way. It's not paranoia, it's awareness. There are people who came back and one Facebook post messed up a job offer. I keep that in mind.

**Q4.4: What frustrates you most about your current plan?**

The data cap. And the way they throttle you before the month ends — I go from LTE to something that barely loads a webpage. I need that last week of the month to be reliable too. Parole doesn't stop because my data did. My job doesn't stop. My group chat doesn't stop. I'm managing everything on my phone and when it slows down I feel it across everything. Also, no one ever calls me by name. I'm a number on an account. When I call support I feel like a transaction.

**Q4.5: Has anything about how you're treated as a phone customer made you feel judged or less than?**

The deposit thing. Every time someone wanted a deposit, I felt it. It's like they're saying: we don't know if you'll pay us, so pay us extra up front to prove it. For someone coming out with nothing, a $150 deposit might as well be $1,500. And even beyond the money — it's the message. You're not trusted here. For people who are trying every day to rebuild trust, to have a company lead with distrust is demoralizing. You learn to expect it and that's the worst part. You stop being surprised.

### Factor 5: Product Landscape and Switching Behavior

**Quantitative context:**

- Current dominant carriers serving the reentry segment: TracFone (Verizon-owned), Boost Mobile, Metro by T-Mobile — all via prepaid, no-contract plans (industry channel analysis; no carrier has a formal reentry program)
- Lifeline carrier market: Q Link Wireless, SafeLink (TracFone-affiliated), Access Wireless — serve the subsidized segment; no identity dimension, no community relationship
- No-credit-check plans available: Mint Mobile, Visible, US Mobile — accessible prepaid, no formal identity or community alignment; the reentry population is not a named segment for any of these brands
- Formal reentry wireless partnerships: Zero documented carrier-reentry organization partnerships in the U.S. as of Q1 2026 (Gather research; confirmed via FICPFM, Safer Foundation, and CEO staff interviews)
- Device access: ~60% of people leaving prison do not have a smartphone at release; they either left one with family or need to acquire one at release (Prison Policy Initiative, 2022)
- Willingness to switch: Intent to switch carriers is maximized by the greenfield starting condition — there is no switching, only acquiring; brand loyalty is not yet established and will form around the first carrier experience

**Interview — 5 questions**

**Q5.1: How did you pick your current carrier and would you switch?**

I didn't really pick. Safer Foundation told me to go to Walgreens and get a prepaid SIM. I got TracFone. It's fine — it works. But if someone came to me through Safer with something that was actually built for people coming back, with a better data plan and no credit check, I'd switch in the same conversation. There's no loyalty to TracFone. I use it because it was the first thing in front of me. A better option would have won me from day one.

**Q5.2: What would make you switch carriers right now — what's the threshold?**

Enough data to not stress about running out. No credit check, no deposit, no contract. A real human I could call if something went wrong — not a bot. And something that felt like it was made for people like me, not just the cheapest thing available for people with no options. I've heard other guys in my group mention SafeLink for Lifeline, but it doesn't feel any different — same generic thing. If Gather or anyone else showed up at Safer and said we work with reentry organizations and built this with returning citizens, that's how you get me.

**Q5.3: Does it matter to you whether a brand has a public stance on second-chance hiring or reentry?**

Very much. I'm starting to be careful about where my money goes. I don't buy from companies that have discriminated against people like me if I can help it. I try to buy from places that are at least trying. If a carrier was actively partnered with Safer, if they had returning citizens on staff or in their ads — that would mean something. I'd tell every person in my group. That's how word spreads in our community. It doesn't start with an ad. It starts with someone trusting it and telling someone else.

**Q5.4: What do you think about the tech experience on your current plan — the app, support, and tools?**

The app is a joke. It tells me how much data I have left and that's basically it. Support is a phone tree that never gets to a human. I've waited 45 minutes to get a question answered about why my service dropped. There's nothing in the app that explains anything. Compare that to banking apps now — they're actually good, they tell you what's happening with your money. Why is my phone carrier so far behind? It feels like they built the app once in 2014 and forgot about it.

**Q5.5: Is there anything a phone carrier could offer as a bundle or extra that would actually matter to your life right now?**

Honest answer? Something connected to background check services or employment verification — like, help me show employers I'm reliable, not just connected. I know that's not really a phone thing. But something in that space. Or even just access to the Second Chance employer network through an app. Resources for returning citizens built into the plan, not an ad for something I'd have to go find myself. Referrals to reentry legal aid. A direct line to my reentry counselor. If the phone plan was actually a tool for coming back, not just a phone plan, that would be different from anything out there.

## Switching Profile Module

### Dimension 1: Trigger Mechanism and Force Score

**Trigger mechanism:** Forced switch (greenfield acquisition variant). The individual exits incarceration with no active wireless plan. There is no incumbent carrier to leave, no number in most cases, and a hard external deadline imposed by parole compliance requirements — connectivity must be established within 24 hours of release.

**Switching force score: 5/5.** Rationale: Score 5 is warranted by the combination of forced acquisition (no plan exists), hard deadline (parole compliance), and complete absence of switching friction (no contract, no ported number, no loyalty, no deposit already paid). The trigger is structural, not attitudinal. The individual is not deciding whether to switch; they are deciding which carrier to acquire on the day of maximum need and minimum optionality.

**Compound trigger mapping:** Two reinforcing triggers fire simultaneously at release. TR-02 (Incarceration Release) creates the immediate wireless need. The MT-09 community formation dynamic creates a parallel identity trigger: returning citizens are actively constructing self-concept in the first weeks and months post-release, making them unusually receptive to brands that signal recognition of that identity. The compound effect is that the initial carrier acquisition is not just a utility decision — it becomes an early identity signal. A carrier associated with trusted reentry organizations is adopted as a community-aligned brand, not just a service.

**Emotional state tag:** Urgent and Overwhelmed. Day of release is cognitively and emotionally saturated — housing, ID, employment, family contact, and parole compliance all compete simultaneously. Wireless is non-negotiable but the capacity to evaluate options is minimal. The channel decision (who presents the option, in what setting) determines the outcome more than the product features do.

**Total annual volume:** ~600K annual releases from state and federal prisons (Bureau of Justice Statistics, 2023). This represents the core greenfield acquisition opportunity per year. The advocate-identity subset (those who will subsequently become active second-chance community members) is estimated at 10–20% of the release population, or 60K–120K per year who are likely to influence community adoption through peer referral.

### Dimension 4: Window Structure

**Window type:** Flash (Day 0–1 acquisition window) embedded within a Short-to-Extended identity window (24–36 months community engagement window). The acquisition moment is Flash — it happens on the day of release and the decision is made in minutes, not days. The identity window for retention and advocacy is significantly longer.

**Peak intent window:** Day 1 post-release for initial acquisition (highest urgency, lowest friction). Days 2–30 for plan confirmation and retention (first 30 days have the highest churn risk as individuals stabilize housing and may encounter financial stress that leads to service interruption). Months 3–12 for advocacy activation — the individual has stabilized and begins actively recommending products to peers in their network.

**Intent decay curve:** Sharp-then-plateau. The Day 1 acquisition urgency is acute and resolves within 24 hours (someone gets a plan or they don't). Intent to stay with the initial plan decays gradually over the first 90 days as competing offers are encountered and financial circumstances evolve. After 90 days, retention stabilizes — the plan becomes part of the routine and churn intent drops significantly unless a specific pain event (outage, billing issue) disrupts.

**Decision journey:**

- **Awareness:** Happens almost entirely through the channel (reentry organization staff recommendation or peer group word-of-mouth). Not through advertising or search — the cognitive load on Day 1 is too high for research.
- **Active search:** Minimal. The decision is primarily: "What can I get right now, here, without a credit check, that will work for parole?" Not a feature comparison.
- **Decision:** Made in the interaction with the reentry staff member or in the first store visit. The recommendation of a trusted intermediary is the decisive factor.
- **Post-decision:** Loyalty forms or erodes in the first 30–90 days based on whether the plan performs reliably under parole compliance conditions. Bad experience in first 30 days leads to churn; good experience in first 30 days creates advocacy.

**Most effective interception point:** The reentry organization interaction, 24–72 hours before or at the point of release. Not post-release consumer marketing — the decision is already made by the time standard marketing channels can reach them.

## Section 3: Executive Brief

### Key Insights

**1. The channel is the product.** For this segment, how the carrier is introduced matters as much as what it offers. A plan distributed through a trusted reentry organization carries a validation signal that no amount of consumer advertising can manufacture. The brand relationship begins with the reentry organization's endorsement, not with the carrier's brand.

**2. Greenfield acquisition with no incumbent is commercially rare.** A Force Score 5 trigger event with zero competitive presence in the channel is exceptional. TracFone and Boost are present at retail but absent at the moment of greatest need (Day 1 post-release through the reentry organization). First-mover in this channel is structurally advantaged — the recommendation becomes the default, and defaults are sticky.

**3. Identity intensity creates viral advocacy.** The returning citizen community is tight-knit, communication-heavy, and recommendation-driven. Marcus's comment — "I'd tell every person in my group" — reflects a real behavioral pattern in the community. Word spreads through peer mentor networks, group support sessions, and reentry organization client communities. A brand that earns community trust does not need paid acquisition to sustain growth.

**4. The compliance use case is the marketing hook.** Most wireless marketing leads with entertainment: streaming, gaming, data speed. For returning citizens, connectivity is a survival mechanism — parole compliance, employment maintenance, family reconnection. Marketing that acknowledges this directly ("built for people who have everything riding on staying connected") would be unprecedented and resonant. No carrier currently speaks this language.

**5. Dignity is the product feature that does not appear on a spec sheet.** Marcus describes deposits, credit checks, and generic service as forms of distrust communicated by carriers. A carrier that eliminates the deposit, removes the credit check, and interacts with returning citizens as valued customers rather than high-risk accounts delivers a feature that competitors cannot quickly copy because it requires institutional will, not technology.

|------|------|------|----------|

| 2 | A carrier that enters the relationship without requiring a deposit or credit check | Stated functional + identity | Q4.5 — "the deposit thing... it's the message"; Q5.2 — explicit switching condition |
| 3 | Recognition from a brand that sees returning citizens as valued community members, not credit risks | Identity aspiration | Q3.5 — "I'd be loyal for life"; Q5.3 — "It doesn't start with an ad. It starts with someone trusting it" |

### Go/No-Go Recommendation

**GO — with B2B channel development prerequisite.**

The commercial case is strong: greenfield acquisition (Force Score 5), no competitive presence in the preferred channel, continuous annual volume (~600K releases per year), and an identity community with unusually high peer-advocacy behavior. The economics are favorable: estimated CPA of $15–$25 through reentry organization partnerships versus $80–$120 industry MVNO average.

The product requirements are achievable at the current infrastructure tier (🟩): no-contract prepaid, no credit check, no deposit, sufficient data for compliance use, WiFi calling for indoor environments, and cash/alternative payment acceptance. No packet core or HSS investment is required for the core product.

**The go/no-go is conditional on one prerequisite:** B2B channel partnerships with reentry organizations must begin in parallel with — not after — Stage 4B product design. Without channel access, the segment is unreachable through consumer marketing alone. The channel is both the distribution mechanism and the trust signal. Gather should begin partner outreach to Safer Foundation, Center for Employment Opportunities, and the Second Chance Business Coalition immediately. Target: at least one signed pilot partnership before public launch.

**Switching profile finding:** The Flash-window interception strategy (Dimension 3, Channel 1) is the primary go-to-market lever. Product design should be optimized for simplicity and speed of activation — the person acquiring on Day 1 cannot navigate a complex onboarding flow. Activation must take less than 5 minutes, require no documentation beyond release paperwork if possible, and result in an active number within the same session.

**Wind-down trigger (per 4B-Rapid requirement, noted here for Stage 4B):** Monitor TracFone/Verizon or Boost/Dish movement into formal reentry organization partnerships. If a competitor secures exclusive channel partnerships with more than three major national reentry organizations, the window advantage narrows sharply. Monitor quarterly.
