## Section 1: Demand Snapshot

### Demand Signal

Social commerce in the United States is undergoing a structural shift from passive product discovery to live, interactive selling events. TikTok Shop, Instagram Live Shopping, and Amazon Live have transformed a subset of content creators into professional live hosts — simultaneously merchants, entertainers, and community managers. This population is defined not by demographics but by a professional role that is entirely dependent on cellular connectivity: they broadcast live, they take audience questions in real time, they process purchases during the stream. A dropped stream is a direct revenue event. A buffered feed loses the audience at the moment of highest purchase intent.

The pain is professional, not cost-driven. This distinguishes P-057 sharply from passive social commerce consumers (P-056, P-058) who are data-heavy but price-sensitive. The Social Commerce Creator / Live Host is willing to pay premium for professional-grade connectivity — and is actively experiencing unmet needs that no current carrier has designed for.

### Trigger

The demand signal emerged from three converging forces: (1) TikTok Shop's US creator program expansion in 2023–2024 brought live commerce to a broader creator pool; (2) Amazon Live's continued growth built an independent professional hosting ecosystem; (3) Instagram Live Shopping codified the format across platforms. Live commerce has moved from early-adopter experiment to recognized revenue channel for mid-tier creators. The trigger is role adoption — when a creator earns meaningful income from live hosting, connectivity becomes professional infrastructure, not consumer spend.

The core addressable population for Gather is the 200K–500K active live hosts who have crossed the threshold into income-generating hosting. This is a concentrated, high-value segment: high data consumption, above-average wireless spend tolerance, and pain that is directly articulable as a connectivity problem.

### Window Duration

**18–36 months.** Social commerce is a genuine micro-trend, not a surge. The platform infrastructure (TikTok Shop, Amazon Live, Instagram Live Shopping) is now stable. The creator supply side is in active expansion. The window closes when one of two things happens: (a) the market matures and hosting becomes a commoditized profession with standardized tooling that commoditizes the underlying connectivity need, or (b) a major carrier explicitly builds for this segment and captures loyalty.

Decay indicators to watch: TikTok regulatory status in the U.S. (potential platform restriction would compress the window), platform fee increases that reduce creator margins (compresses WTP for premium wireless), creator economy consolidation toward agency models (shifts connectivity purchasing from individual creator to agency).

## Section 2: Core Factor Analysis

### Persona: Mia Chen, 29, Los Angeles, CA

Mia hosts three live shopping events per week on TikTok Shop — primarily beauty and fashion — plus occasional Amazon Live sessions for brand partnership deals. She started in 2022 as a content creator and shifted toward live commerce in early 2024 when she realized a single 2-hour stream could generate more revenue than a week of static posts. She earns $60,000–$90,000/year from live commerce, split between commission income and brand partnerships. She lives in a 1-bedroom apartment in Koreatown, runs her streams from a ring-lit corner of her living room, and manages her entire operation from a MacBook and two iPhones (one for streaming, one for monitoring comments). She is on a major carrier postpaid plan at $85/month. She has had two stream drops in the last three months — one cost her approximately $800 in lost sales.

### Factor 2: Spending and Financial Habits

**Quantitative Context:**

Creator economy workers earn variable income with strong upside for top performers. Full-time social commerce creators earning through TikTok Shop report average monthly revenue of $3,000–$8,000 from commission income, with top 10% exceeding $15,000/month (Creator IQ / TikTok internal data reported by Influencer Marketing Hub, 2025). A single dropped stream during a peak product launch can cost 20–40% of the event's projected revenue (estimated from creator community reports on r/TikTokShop and community Discord servers).

On wireless spend, professional creators report treating connectivity as business overhead: 68% of creator economy workers claim a wireless plan as a business deduction (Freshbooks Self-Employment Study, 2024). Monthly wireless spend for professional creators averages $95–$135/month when including hotspot-enabled business plans (PCMag MVNO Price Survey, 2025). Willingness to pay for professional-grade performance — specifically guaranteed upload speeds and stream reliability — is elevated relative to general consumers: 34% of content creators surveyed said they would pay $20–$40/month more for "professional streaming quality" (Civic Science Creator Survey, 2025).

**Interview: Factor 2 — Spending and Financial Habits**

**Q2.1: How do you think about your wireless plan in terms of your work expenses?**

> Honestly, my phone plan is just cost of doing business at this point — same as my ring light and my ring lite fill. I write it off. When I had that drop in December, I lost probably eight hundred bucks in one night. So if there's a plan that's thirty dollars more a month but never drops a stream, that math is so obvious to me. That's not even two bad nights.

**Q2.2: What do you currently spend on wireless, and do you feel like you're getting value?**

> I'm at eighty-five a month right now on AT&T. Sometimes it feels fine, sometimes I want to throw my phone across the room mid-stream. The value question is tricky — I'm getting average, I think. Nobody's ever offered me something that was actually built for what I do. It feels like I'm using a regular consumer product for a professional job.

**Q2.3: Are there other digital tools you pay for to support your streaming business, and how does wireless fit into that ecosystem?**

> I pay for StreamYard, I pay for Later for scheduling, I have a TikTok Shop analytics tool — probably two hundred a month in SaaS stuff. Wireless is actually the single most important one and somehow the one I've put the least thought into. I just signed up for AT&T a few years ago. I've never actually shopped my plan the way I've shopped every other tool I use.

**Q2.4: If a plan specifically for live commerce creators existed, what would make it worth a price premium?**

> It would need to prove itself, right? Like, show me the uptime data. Show me upload speed guarantees. Don't just say "unlimited" — every carrier says unlimited. I want to see "guaranteed five megabit uplink during live events" and I want someone to actually stand behind that. If that's real, I'd probably pay a hundred and twenty, maybe a hundred and thirty a month without blinking.

**Q2.5: How do you handle income variability, and does that affect how you think about subscription costs?**

> My income swings a lot month to month. Good months are really good. But I've learned to think in averages — my plan cost is fixed, my income is variable. So I actually prefer to pay a little more for something that works every time rather than save twenty bucks and have the thing fail at the worst moment. I budget for reliability.

### Factor 3: Needs and Aspirations

**Quantitative Context:**

The live commerce hosting profession is in active formalization. 74% of social commerce creators report treating their work as a primary business rather than a side hustle (NRF Social Commerce Study, 2024). Professional identity formation is explicit: TikTok Shop has introduced tiered "creator levels" with platform badges, and Amazon Live provides hosting certifications. These professional markers create identity stakes around the role.

Stated needs center on upload reliability — U.S. mobile upload speeds average 8–12 Mbps on LTE/5G nationally, but MVNO subscribers are deprioritized during congestion, and live streaming requires sustained 5–10 Mbps upload with low jitter (Opensignal U.S. Mobile Network Experience Report, 2025). The gap between "what is required for professional hosting" and "what a standard consumer carrier delivers" is widest precisely during peak streaming hours (evenings, weekends) when congestion is highest.

Unstated needs run deeper: live hosts want to be taken seriously as professionals, not treated as heavy data consumers. The identity aspiration is "professional broadcaster," not "content creator who uses a lot of data." No carrier has ever spoken to them in that language.

**Interview: Factor 3 — Needs and Aspirations**

**Q3.1: When you imagine your streaming setup working perfectly, what does that actually look like?**

> I never think about my connection. That's what perfect looks like — it's invisible. Right now I have this low-level anxiety every single stream, like checking the signal bar in the corner of my eye every few minutes. Perfect is when I'm one hundred percent in the room with my audience, talking product, building that connection, and connectivity is just not a thing I'm aware of at all.

**Q3.2: How do you think about your professional identity — do you see yourself as a content creator, a business owner, or something else?**

> I've started saying "live commerce host" when people ask what I do, because "content creator" makes people think I'm posting dance videos. I'm actually running a broadcast business. I have brand partners, I have inventory relationships, I have a production schedule. A content creator posts pictures. I host live events. That distinction matters to me — I want the tools I use to understand that distinction too.

**Q3.3: What would it mean to you to have a wireless provider that was specifically built for people like you?**

> It sounds kind of cheesy but — validation? Like, someone finally sees what I'm doing as a real profession. Every other tool I use is built for my job. My ring light, my mic, my analytics software — all for creators. My phone plan is just... a thing I have. If there was a plan that said, this was built for people hosting live events, you'd have my attention immediately.

**Q3.4: Where do you see your live commerce business in two years, and what would need to be true about your tech setup?**

> I want to be doing five, maybe six streams a week, doing more brand partnerships, maybe having an assistant helping with comments. I want to start streaming from different locations — events, pop-ups, not just my apartment. And that's where the connectivity thing gets scary, because right now I depend on my home WiFi as backup. If I'm mobile, I'm one hundred percent on cellular. That's a different risk level.

**Q3.5: Is there a gap between how you feel about your work and how you think others see it?**

> My family still doesn't fully get it. They're supportive, but they'll say something like "oh you're still doing the TikTok thing?" And I'm like, I made more last month than my cousin who has a real job. I think about that when I'm buying tools for my business — I want to buy things that are professional. Not because of them, honestly. Because of how I want to see myself.

### Factor 4: Fears and Pain Points

**Quantitative Context:**

Stream drops are the primary pain vector. On mobile networks, MVNO subscribers experience deprioritization during peak hours that can reduce upload throughput by 50–80% relative to the MNO's own postpaid customers (AT&T MVNO wholesale QCI framework, as documented in performance-features.md). Live streaming at 1080p requires sustained 5 Mbps uplink; a drop below 2 Mbps causes visible quality degradation; below 500 Kbps, the stream buffering becomes audience-visible.

The economic exposure is real: a mid-stream drop during a product launch can result in a 20–40% reduction in per-event revenue, per creator community reports. For a creator earning $5,000–$8,000/month from live commerce, this represents a meaningful loss. Additionally, platform algorithms penalize hosts for stream quality issues — TikTok Shop's creator ranking system considers completion rate and stream quality, meaning technical failures compound into reduced platform visibility (TikTok Creator Academy Documentation, 2025).

Secondary fears include: device failure with no backup provisioned, loss of audience trust through repeated technical issues, and being locked out of prime-time streaming slots by platform scheduling systems that deprioritize unreliable hosts.

**Interview: Factor 4 — Fears and Pain Points**

**Q4.1: Walk me through what happens when your stream drops — technically and emotionally.**

> It's awful. The stream just cuts, and for a second you don't know if it's you or TikTok. Then you're frantically trying to reconnect while all those people you spent twenty minutes warming up are gone. Some come back but most don't. And then there's this shame spiral — I keep thinking my audience is watching and thinking I'm unprofessional. Even though it's the network's fault. I still feel it.

**Q4.2: What aspects of your connectivity do you feel most uncertain about, and how does that affect how you plan your streams?**

> I won't go live at eight PM on a Friday anymore. That sounds crazy but I literally rescheduled my streams around when I think the network is less congested. I do Tuesday afternoons, Thursday evenings. That's my production schedule now — built around avoiding peak network hours. Nobody ever told me I'd have to think about that as a live commerce host.

**Q4.3: Have you ever lost a brand partnership or audience trust because of a technical failure?**

> I almost lost one. A brand sent me product for a Saturday launch and I had a rough stream with some buffering — not a full drop but choppy. They didn't say anything officially, but the renewal came in lower. I think about that constantly. Because my entire professional relationship with brands is built on me delivering a quality event. My phone plan is the weakest link in that chain.

**Q4.4: What would have to happen to make you feel genuinely unsafe about your streaming business?**

> Honestly, if TikTok goes away that's a different conversation. But in terms of what keeps me up at night right now — a major event where I go live for something big, thousands of people, and I drop at the worst moment. Like, go live for a product launch I've been building for three weeks and the stream dies at peak buying time. That would be devastating. I think about that before every big event.

**Q4.5: What do you do to try to compensate for unreliable connectivity today?**

> I keep my home WiFi as the primary and my phone hotspot as backup — sort of. But the hotspot is throttled after ten gigs on my current plan, so it's not really a safety net, it's a band-aid. I've started checking the TikTok signal thing before every stream. I set up a speed test shortcut on my home screen. I do a test stream five minutes before going live. I've built a whole pre-stream ritual around compensating for uncertainty I shouldn't have to have.

### Factor 5: Product Landscape and Switching Behavior

**Quantitative Context:**

Current plan landscape: major carrier postpaid plans (AT&T, Verizon, T-Mobile) range $70–$90/month for single unlimited lines. MVNOs range $25–$60/month. No carrier or MVNO has a creator-specific or live-streaming-specific plan architecture. US Mobile's "Super LTE" plan at $44/month includes prioritized data and is marketed to data-heavy users, but has no creator identity framing. Visible Party Pay (Verizon MVNO) offers $25/month plans but with explicit deprioritization policies that are incompatible with professional streaming needs.

Switching behavior: professional creators have moderately high switching propensity driven by performance, not price. Stage 3 Carrier Mobility score: 4/5 for P-057. Of creator economy workers surveyed, 47% said they had considered switching carriers specifically due to streaming performance issues (Civic Science Creator Survey, 2025). Switching barriers are low: number portability is mature, eSIM simplifies activation, and the persona has no family plan complexity (individual professional plan).

**Interview: Factor 5 — Product Landscape and Switching Behavior**

**Q5.1: How do you currently choose your wireless plan, and when did you last actually compare options?**

> I signed up for AT&T when I got my first iPhone and I've sort of just... stayed. I upgraded plans twice but never actually switched carriers. I've looked at Mint Mobile because Ryan Reynolds is funny, but when I actually read the fine print on deprioritization, I closed the tab. I need someone to tell me what their upload speeds actually are during peak hours. Nobody puts that in their marketing.

**Q5.2: What would it take for you to switch your carrier today?**

> At this point, performance proof. Specific numbers — "we guarantee five megabits upload during live events or we credit your bill." Something testable. I could switch in an afternoon — my setup is just me, one line. There's no family plan to unravel. The friction isn't the mechanics of switching, it's that no carrier has given me a reason that's specific to my situation.

**Q5.3: What plans or brands have you looked at, and what's made you hesitant?**

> I looked at US Mobile for a hot minute because people on r/TikTokShop were talking about it. The price is better but I couldn't find clear data on upload reliability. T-Mobile's creator plans felt like marketing, not engineering — they had that T-Mobile for Content Creators thing but it was basically "we partnered with some stock photo site," which tells me they don't understand what I actually need. Verizon has the best reputation for reliability but eighty-five dollars for one line feels like I'm paying for a brand, not a product.

**Q5.4: Are there any features that would make you genuinely excited about a carrier — something that would make you tell other creators?**

> Oh yes — if there was a plan that had a stream reliability dashboard. Like, you open the app and it says "your upload averaged five-point-eight megabits over your last six streams, here are the two nights where congestion affected you." That's data I actually want. That's something I'd screenshot and share in my creator group chat. Right now I have zero visibility into what my network is actually doing during my streams.

**Q5.5: If a wireless plan designed specifically for live commerce hosts existed, what would make you skeptical of it?**

> If the "creator features" are just branding. Like, a phone case discount or a logo on the website. I've been burned by that kind of thing — products that say they're for creators and then it's just a rebrand of a regular consumer product. What I'd look for is: does the technical architecture actually support what I need? Is there a service-level commitment on upload speeds? Can I actually talk to support and say "my stream dropped" and have someone who understands what that means?

## Section 3: Executive Brief

### Key Insights

**Insight 1: The pain is professional, and no competitor speaks its language.** P-057's connectivity need is materially different from a general data-heavy consumer. The 200K–500K live hosts in this segment are running broadcast businesses on infrastructure designed for consumer use. Their complaints — throttled upload, stream drops during peak hours, MVNO deprioritization — are professional failures, not inconveniences. The Stage 3 data confirms: Pain & Demand scores 4/5 and no current carrier or MVNO has designed a product, a brand, or even a marketing message that addresses the live host's professional context.

**Insight 2: Uplink is the structural gap, and it is solvable.** Standard cellular architecture is asymmetric — download-heavy, upload-constrained. Live streaming is the inverse: it is entirely upload-dependent. A 1080p live stream requires sustained 5–10 Mbps uplink with low jitter and low packet loss (performance-features.md, P1.4). MVNO deprioritization during peak hours pushes uplink below 500 Kbps — causing audience-visible stream degradation. Gather's differentiated position: guaranteed uplink for live events, implemented via CAMARA QoD API (symmetric quality class, feasible at the 🟢 infrastructure tier) or PCF-based uplink priority scheduling (🟡 tier). This is not a messaging problem — it is a product architecture problem that Gather can address.

**Insight 3: The switching propensity is high and the friction is low.** Carrier Mobility scored 4/5. This persona is a solo professional on an individual plan, no family plan entanglement, eSIM-ready, and already frustrated with existing performance. The switching barrier is not mechanics — it is evidence. Mia's words: "Show me the uplink data. Show me the guarantee." A plan that can produce verifiable stream reliability numbers can convert this segment efficiently. CAC advantage is also real: the social commerce creator community has strong peer-recommendation behavior — a single converted creator with a good experience will show the data to their creator group chat.

**Insight 4: Professional identity is a retention lever, not just an acquisition hook.** The aspiration to be seen as a "live commerce host" rather than a "content creator who uses a lot of data" is emotionally active. A carrier that validates this professional identity creates loyalty that goes beyond plan features. When Mia says "validation" is what she would feel from a creator-native carrier, she is describing an emotional need that Mint Mobile, Visible, and US Mobile are constitutionally incapable of meeting. They are price brands. Gather is an identity brand.

**Insight 5: Transparency is the product that sells the product.** The feature Mia would screenshot and share — a stream reliability dashboard with per-session uplink averages — is entirely within MVNO app capability at the 🟩 infrastructure tier (device APIs + CAMARA Connectivity Insights). This is not a difficult product to ship. It is a trust-building artifact that no carrier currently offers and that the creator community would amplify organically.

### Go/No-Go Recommendation

**GO.**

The INVEST quadrant assignment is well-earned. The combination of high switching propensity (Carrier Mobility: 4), real and articulated professional pain (Pain & Demand: 4), a clear and unserved identity (Identity Intensity: 4), and a competitive landscape where no carrier or MVNO has made a targeted move (P2: 4.25 — Joint Gaps: 5, Differentiation Potential: 5) creates a rare alignment.

The technical feasibility is actionable at multiple infrastructure tiers:
- At the 🟩 tier: stream reliability dashboard in the app, WiFi calling default provisioning, pre-stream speed test integration
- At the 🟢 tier: CAMARA QoD API (symmetric quality class) for upload-prioritized sessions during live events
- At the 🟡 tier: PCF uplink priority scheduling and guaranteed uplink floor for subscribers in active stream sessions

The 18–36 month window is sufficient to build and launch. The secondary persona (P-095: Livestream Shopping Host Building Brand) provides an upsell path — newer hosts with lower income and higher price sensitivity who grow into the full-featured plan as their business matures.

**Speed-to-market requirement:** The TikTok platform regulatory environment introduces a window compression risk. Any delay beyond 6–9 months may reduce the addressable population if platform-level disruption contracts the TikTok Shop creator program. Prioritize an MVP that leads with the reliability narrative and stream dashboard, and can be positioned across Amazon Live and Instagram Live Shopping as platform diversification.

**Confidence modifier note:** Confidence scored 3 (medium). The population estimate (200K–500K) is derived from extrapolation rather than direct survey data. Stage 5 survey validation should confirm willingness to pay at the $100–$130/month price tier and test the stream reliability dashboard feature as a switching motivator before full product investment.
